# Pattern Library

A house library of ad-craft patterns for short software ads. Each pattern has an ID, a one-line description, when to use it, and rules. The builder picks patterns by ID when writing the script and storyboard, so every creative choice is traceable to a named rule.

Patterns are grouped by the job they do. Nothing here is tied to a brand. Colors are described by role, never by a specific hex value, because the product's own brand spec supplies the actual colors.

## How the builder uses this file

1. Read the playbook chosen in the questionnaire. It lists a default set of pattern IDs.
2. Read the audience research summary, if present. Pain-led audiences favor problem hooks and proof devices. Aspiration-led audiences favor product-world hooks and breadth structures.
3. Pick one hook, one story structure, one color strategy, one composition family, three to five motion moves, one voiceover register, one call to action style, and one to three trust devices.
4. List the chosen IDs in SCRIPT.md under "Patterns used." If a scene breaks a pattern rule, say why in the storyboard.

## A. Hooks (first 1 to 3 seconds)

The hook has one job: stop the scroll or survive the skip button. Pick one.

**A1. Pain question**
A single question that names the viewer's frustration, on a minimal dark or flat frame with the brand mark. Then the frame bursts into color as the answer arrives.
When: problem-led ads, sales-led offers, buyers who feel a daily operational pain.
Rules: one question, under eight words. Hold still for about one second. The color burst that follows is the emotional pivot, so make it decisive.

**A2. Typewriter line**
A short line types out character by character with a blinking cursor on a near-empty frame.
When: sound-off placements, text-driven ads, any ad that opens with a promise rather than a problem.
Rules: the line must be readable in under two seconds once complete. Use the cursor to mimic the act of working. Pair with a second beat that finishes the thought.

**A3. Empty color**
The frame opens as pure color or gradient with nothing on it, then the brand mark and a cursor fade in.
When: awareness ads in busy feeds, brands with a recognizable color.
Rules: the empty hold lasts about one second. The cursor implies something is about to be clicked.

**A4. Inside the product world**
Open already inside a composed scene of the product: cards, panels, and data floating on a clean canvas, with the voiceover naming the brand in the first second.
When: narrated demos, products with a rich interface, aspiration-led messages.
Rules: no logo intro. Visual hook and verbal hook land together. The scene should read as a designed poster, not a screen recording.

**A5. Brand mark assembly**
The brand mark builds from pieces that fly in and lock together, then morphs into the first product element.
When: brands with a distinctive mark, awareness ads, any ad that wants a seamless bridge from brand to product.
Rules: keep it under two seconds. The morph must land on something the viewer can use, not on more branding.

**A6. Sensory misdirection**
Open on non-software imagery that belongs to a customer's world, with a small brand watermark.
When: customer stories, products used by companies with a physical or visual business. Requires real footage.
Rules: the watermark is the only brand element. Resolve the misdirection within three seconds.

**A7. White space**
A single centered element on pure white.
When: feeds dominated by dark or saturated content.
Rules: only one element. Motion enters after the still beat, not before.

## B. Story structures

The spine of the ad. Pick one.

**B1. Four acts: problem, solution, proof, offer**
Feel the pain, see the answer, watch it work, act now.
When: most 30 second ads. The default for sales-led software.
Rules: rough split for 30 seconds is 5, 8, 12, 5. Proof gets the most time.

**B2. Breadth to depth**
Show several examples, pull back to show many, then push in to one hero moment that proves it is live.
When: platforms that serve many teams or use cases, aspiration-led narration.
Rules: three examples is the minimum that reads as a list. Save one real interface animation for the final push-in.

**B3. Progressive complexity**
Open on the simplest possible frame, then add one element per beat until the full system is visible.
When: products whose value is connecting many things, longer ads.
Rules: each new element must represent a distinct capability. Stop before the frame gets crowded.

**B4. Promise then prove**
The opening line makes one promise. The very next scene shows the product doing exactly that.
When: single-feature ads, 15 second ads.
Rules: one promise only. The product scene must enact the promise literally, not illustrate it loosely.

**B5. Bookend**
Open and close on the same branded frame.
When: any ad. Stacks with any other structure.
Rules: same background, same mark position, same type treatment. The closer adds the call to action.

**B6. One idea**
The whole ad communicates exactly one thing.
When: 15 second ads, sound-off ads.
Rules: no feature lists, no second benefit. If a scene does not serve the one idea, cut it.

## C. Color strategies

How color carries meaning. The product's brand spec supplies the actual values.

**C1. Two-world pivot**
Dark or muted for the problem, bright and saturated for the solution. The switch is the emotional turn.
When: problem-led ads with A1 hooks.
Rules: the pivot happens once, early, and hard. Do not drift back to the dark palette until the closer.

**C2. Dark bookends, bright middle**
Dark branded frames open and close the ad. The content section is bright.
When: premium or enterprise tone that still wants to feel approachable.
Rules: the dark frames carry only the mark, the headline, and the call to action.

**C3. One accent per section**
Each section takes a different saturated accent as its background. Sections change with a hard cut.
When: ads that walk through several use cases or feature areas.
Rules: the accent lives in the background, not on the cards. Cards stay white or neutral so the interface reads.

**C4. Calm cards, loud canvas**
White or neutral cards on a canvas that carries the color.
When: any composed product-world scene.
Rules: only the headline numbers and section labels are allowed to be loud inside the cards.

**C5. Branded frame**
A persistent branded gradient or color border around every interface shot and every person shot.
When: ads that mix interface with footage, or that want brand recognition without a persistent logo.
Rules: the frame is the only persistent brand element. No logo header.

**C6. One keyword in accent**
On any text card, exactly one word is set in the accent color.
When: text-driven ads, sound-off ads.
Rules: the accented word is the one the viewer should remember.

## D. Composition families

How the frame is arranged.

**D1. Floating cards**
Interface cards scattered on a clean canvas with deliberate offset, soft shadows, and generous margins.
When: product-world scenes, narrated demos.
Rules: cards may overlap and may be slightly rotated. Thin connector lines can imply flow. Never a grid.

**D2. Focal point with orbit**
One central card or device with satellite elements arranged around it, each standing for a capability.
When: showing breadth at a glance, ecosystem or integration stories.
Rules: each satellite represents something real. Add satellites one at a time.

**D3. Split screen personas**
Two panels, two colors, two roles, each with a short quote or workflow.
When: products that serve more than one team, longer ads.
Rules: both panels read at the same time. Text in each panel is one line.

**D4. Conversation card**
A chat or request-and-response exchange rendered as a floating card.
When: products with an assistant, a request flow, or a ticket flow. Works without screenshots.
Rules: the request is in natural language. The response includes one unexpectedly specific detail that proves understanding.

**D5. Feature pills**
Icon plus short label in a white pill. Pills stack in one at a time.
When: listing three to five capabilities fast. Works without screenshots.
Rules: five pills maximum. Each pill gets its own beat. Labels are benefits, not technical names.

**D6. Metrics card**
A dashboard-style card with a live status badge, two or three improvement numbers, and small avatars.
When: the proof act. Works without screenshots.
Rules: numbers must be real or clearly illustrative. Never invent a customer metric.

**D7. Big text card**
Three to seven words, very large, on a plain background.
When: sound-off ads, problem statements, taglines.
Rules: one idea per card. Readable at arm's length on a phone.

**D8. Workflow card**
A vertical or horizontal chain of steps with icons and arrows, showing a trigger and what follows.
When: automation, routing, approvals. Works without screenshots.
Rules: three to four steps. Real tool icons only if the integrations are real.

**D9. Person plus interface**
Footage of a real person alternating with interface shots.
When: customer stories. Requires footage.
Rules: never more than two consecutive shots of either kind.

## E. Motion vocabulary

Every scene must move. Pick three to five moves for the whole ad and repeat them, so the ad has a consistent motion language.

**E1. Hard cut on sentence boundary**
Scenes change exactly where the voiceover sentence or clause ends.
Rules: no fades between sections. Cuts feel decisive.

**E2. Card cascade**
Several cards slide in from off-screen one after another with a small stagger and a tiny overshoot.
Rules: stagger of 100 to 200 ms. Nothing wobbles.

**E3. Camera pull-back and push-in**
The view scales out to reveal many, then scales in to land on one.
Rules: pure scale, no rotation. Land on the hero element exactly as the key phrase is spoken.

**E4. One punchline animation**
The single real interface animation in the ad, saved for the moment the voiceover says the key word.
Rules: everything else is composition change. This one moment carries the "it is live" argument.

**E5. Typewriter text**
Text types in character by character.
Rules: even pacing. Use for hooks and feature labels, not body copy.

**E6. Cursor as narrator**
An animated pointer moves through the interface and clicks.
Rules: human pace, not robotic. Desktop products only.

**E7. Organic wipe**
A curved color shape sweeps across to change scenes.
Rules: use when hard cuts feel too harsh for the brand. Keep the arc consistent in direction.

**E8. Flash frame**
One full-frame flash of the primary brand color before the closer.
Rules: two to four frames only.

**E9. Slow pan or scroll over a screenshot**
Wide content pans horizontally, tall content scrolls.
Rules: never show a screenshot static. Pre-crop instead of scaling with code.

**E10. Stillness on the closer**
The final frame does not move.
Rules: two to four seconds of hold after the motion. The contrast sells the action.

## F. Pacing rules

- One beat is two to three seconds. A 15 second ad has five to seven beats. A 30 second ad has ten to fourteen. A 59 second ad has twenty to twenty-six and needs a mid-point re-hook.
- The hook holds still for about one second before anything moves.
- Pace accelerates into the middle and slows on the payoff.
- The closer holds for two to four seconds. Shorter for 15 second ads.
- Text cards hold long enough to read twice, roughly 1.5 to 2.5 seconds for five to seven words.
- A human read runs at 2.5 to 2.7 words per second. Faster sounds like a hard sell, slower sounds like a training video. Generated voices measure closer to 2.1 with pauses, so plan word counts at 2.1.
- For skippable placements, the brand name and the core promise both land before the five second mark.

## G. Voiceover craft

**G1. Register: calm and confident**
Measured newsreader pace, lower register, no urgency. The default for buyers of business software.

**G2. Register: warm and conversational**
Slightly playful, uses "you," allows a light aside. For products that want to feel human.

**G3. Single continuous sentence**
The whole ad is one grammatical sentence with no pauses. For ads under 15 seconds.

**G4. Sentence bridging**
Start a sentence in one scene and finish it in the next. Pulls the viewer across the cut.

**G5. Single-word pivots**
"Now?" or "Not anymore." Vary sentence length for rhythm.

**G6. Literal sync**
When the voice says the key verb, the visual does that verb.

**G7. One clause per scene**
Each scene gets its own clause. No capability is spoken while a different one is on screen.

**G8. Question in, imperative out**
Open with a question if problem-led. Close with a plain instruction.

**G9. Never read the address**
The web address lives on the closer visually. It is never spoken.

**G10. Marketing rhythm**
Sounds like a confident person talking, not a list being read. Use bridging, pivots, varied length, and casual payoffs.

## H. Copy and call to action

**H1. Category statement closer**
A short declarative line that claims a category, then the mark.

**H2. Two-tier call to action**
A headline that names the offer plus a button that names the action.

**H3. Sales-led action**
"Talk to sales," "Book a demo," "Schedule a walkthrough." For enterprise and mid-market buyers where the purchase is a team decision.

**H4. Self-serve action**
"Start free," "Get started." For products with a trial.

**H5. Offer layering**
Value line first, then the price or promotion line in the same frame.

**H6. No caveats**
No "limited time," no "no credit card required" in the spoken line. Just the action.

**H7. Headline styles**
Benefit, curiosity, category claim, or problem. Pick one per ad and keep it consistent.

## I. Trust devices

Pick one to three. Every device must be true.

**I1. Single hard number**
One metric, full-screen, given its own beat. One memorable number beats five forgettable ones.

**I2. Live status and metrics card**
A card that looks like a running dashboard with an active badge and improvement numbers. See D6.

**I3. Integration marks**
Logos of tools the product genuinely connects to.

**I4. Security signals**
A shield, a lock, an audit or control label. Essential for buyers who own risk.

**I5. Realistic data density**
Cards full of plausible names, dates, and amounts, so the interface looks like someone's actual workspace.

**I6. Named person**
A real person with name, title, and company in a lower third. Requires footage.

**I7. Social proof count**
A customer or user count placed as the beat right before the call to action.

**I8. Polish as credibility**
No claims at all. The quality of the composition is the proof.

**I9. Breadth signal**
Several teams, industries, or use cases shown in sequence, implying wide adoption.

## J. Music and mix

- Continuous music bed under the whole ad. Steady energy, no drops.
- Ducked to about 18 percent under voice. Fade out 1.2 seconds before the end.
- Optional lift at the moment of peak visual complexity and again at the call to action.
- Optional near-silence on the closer hold for text-driven ads.
- Loudness target minus 14 to minus 16 LUFS integrated for social and web placements. True peak at or below minus 1 dBTP.
- No sound effects on card entrances unless the brand spec asks for them.

---

## K. Anti-patterns

- Voiceover that reads like a feature list.
- Captions or subtitles on product scenes that repeat the voiceover.
- A screenshot shown static.
- A logo header or address on every scene.
- Reading the web address aloud.
- Stock footage in a customer story.
- Borrowing another brand's palette, mascot, or signature shape.
- More than one hard number in a 15 or 30 second ad.
- A call to action that appears before the proof act.
- A 59 second ad without a re-hook near the midpoint.
- Three hook variants that are the same idea reworded. Each variant uses a different hook pattern.
- A cutdown made by speeding up the master. Rewrite the lines shorter instead.

## L. Selection guide

**By goal**
- Awareness: A3 or A5 hook, B5 or B6 structure, H1 closer, I8 or I9.
- Demo or sales lead: A1 hook, B1 structure, C1 or C2 color, D6 proof, H3 action, I1 plus I4.
- Trial signup: A4 hook, B2 or B4 structure, C3 color, E4 punchline, H4 action.

**By sound**
- Sound on: G1 or G2 register, G7, E1. Voice carries the message, visuals prove it.
- Sound off: A2 hook, D7 cards, C6 accent keyword, E5, no voiceover, H1 closer.

**By length**
- 15 seconds: B4 or B6, G3, one trust device, closer of two to three seconds.
- 30 seconds: B1, G7, two trust devices, closer of three to four seconds.
- 59 seconds: B1 or B3, a second hook near the midpoint, D3 or D8 for depth, closer of four seconds.

**By hook variant set**
A good set of three bets on three different things: a pain (A1), a proof (the number as opener, I1), and a moment (the conversation as opener, D4). For sound-off ads swap A1 for A2.

**By available material**
- With screenshots: D1, E9, E4, I5.
- Without screenshots: D4, D5, D6, D7, D8, E5, E2. Illustrated cards and typography carry the product story. See production rules for the no-screenshot mode.
